The AI capex race just crossed $700B+, and the four companies fronting the bulk of it are beginning to ask themselves the same question: will it pay off?

In 2026, Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, and Meta have collectively invested over $700B+ in AI capex, where last year it was just around $410B+. However, not much of these investments have come into fruition. For starters, Alphabet, the parent owning company of Google, just received its first ever negative Free Cash Flow statement. This provides a lot of questions in the public eye whether this investment was a strong move, however sophisticated investors are leaning towards that it was not. When Alphabet raised their capex guidance, investors did not respond with the usual reward of buying in - they sold. That is a tell: a guidance raise is supposed to signal rising confidence in future returns and the market's response signaled the opposite.

Alphabet has begun to raise anxiety among the other companies as well. Forbes' Jon Markman covered, Microsoft is beginning to shift their behavior, quietly building in-house AI models to reduce their reliance on OpenAI and Anthropic, despite holding major stakes in both. It is a strange move, hedging against the companies you're betting on.

Looking into where the risk on AI is paying off, AWS had a margin expansion of 520 bps and a $25B+ run rate. However AWS is one sector inside Amazon, a company committing roughly $220B+ of CapEx this year. A great quarter in one sector is not telling you that the company is doing well holistically, margin gains in one area can mask the cash burn everywhere else.

Google Cloud's $514 billion backlog is real, it is solid revenue from customers. However, the backlog is not cash, it is revenue that has not been collected yet, some of it being years out. Meanwhile, Alphabet is spending heavily to build the infrastructure to follow through on those contracts. Which is exactly how a company can be sitting on record demand and posting negative free cash flow at the same time. This backlog proves that customers believe in the bet, it just hasn't paid off yet.

There is a lot of uncertainty of what happens next, but as Jim Covello, head of Global Equity Research at Goldman Sachs, puts it,

> "The economics of AI are more questionable today than they were two years ago."

There is a strong chance that these investments will prove themselves, but as of currently, they have not.
