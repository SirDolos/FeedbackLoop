Germany is currently under massive economic stress that has compounded many different factors. There is no single issue, as in all of economics, but rather a combination of outside and internal factors. The graph below exhibits the real GDP growth vs Inflation.

It is important to note the success from 2015 to 2019: annual inflation stayed below 2% every year, and real GDP growth stayed positive, averaging around 1.7% a year. In 2020, Covid hit and Germany contracted 4.1%, the second worst annual decline in the IMF's data going back to 1980, trailing only the 2009 financial crisis. 2021 was a rebound, with GDP growing 3.9%, driven by open supply chains and exports, however it is more important to look at the rising inflation. Inflation climbed to 3.2% that year, the first sign of what was coming.

The main driver of inflation was caused by Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The western world quickly backed Ukraine by sanctioning trade with Russia. In response, Russia limited exports in natural gas and oil, and by August 2022 had cut flows through Nord Stream 1, the gas pipeline to Germany, down to zero. Energy prices spiked, and Germany suffered. Roughly 41% of Germany's GDP comes from exports, so the massive increase in energy prices caused costs to surge across its industrial base. Inflation peaked at 8.7% in 2022, the highest annual rate in the IMF's data on Germany since 1980, before easing to 6% in 2023.

In 2023 the European Central Bank tried to combat the high inflation and hiked 200 basis points from 2% to 4%. The citizens paid the price, and as energy prices stayed elevated, there was intense pressure on the economy. In 2024, the election of the United States was forthcoming, with Donald Trump causing macro stress in Europe due to promised tariffs. At the same time, China was expanding their exports into more markets, most importantly in this case, automobiles.

In 2025, Donald Trump came into office and quickly implemented 50% tariffs on the European Union, Germany included, for metal and steel exports, and set 15% tariffs on most all goods. Across all sectors, German exports to the United States fell nearly 8% year on year in the first three quarters of 2025, a sharp reversal from years of steady growth.

The impact of tariffs on Germany was not limited to the European Union. Trump released harsh tariffs on China, and as a result China increased manufacturing in all massive exporting markets. As tension intensified between the US and China, China began deteriorating trade partnerships with US allies including Germany. The United States remains Germany's largest single export market, while China ranks among its largest trading partners overall. At the same time, China is now producing and exporting automobiles that significantly underprice the German market. German automobiles made up just under 17% of exports as of 2019, while also creating a web of domestic manufacturers that directly employs about 732,000 people, roughly 1.6% of Germany's total workforce. Auto exports have slowed sharply, dropping to 225,300 units in January 2026 and 271,300 in February 2026, well below the levels the industry ran at before the pandemic. Recently Volkswagen announced they are planning to cut as many as 100,000 jobs and close up to four factories in Germany, a sharp escalation from the 35,000 job, no closures deal it struck with unions back in December 2024. They are also projecting sales revenue to fall by as much as 3% this year. Volkswagen is the largest German automobile company and reflects the market as a whole. VW CEO Oliver Blume has repeatedly pointed to intensifying Chinese competition, warning that Chinese manufacturers are building highly efficient factories in Europe that German plants cannot match.

Taken together, Germany's stress is not a single wound but the same vulnerability struck repeatedly. The export engine that drove the strong, low inflation growth of 2015 to 2019 became the channel through which every shock since has traveled: Covid, the energy crisis, the rate hikes to tame inflation, and now the twin squeeze of US tariffs and Chinese competition on the auto sector that anchors a meaningful share of German manufacturing jobs. The cyclical shocks are fading, but the structural question remains: can German industry stay competitive without cheap energy and with China now a rival rather than its best customer?
